# deslop-text rulebook

A deduplicated guide to structural and word-level AI-writing tells. The structural patterns are ranked strongest first. A pattern's presence is evidence to inspect, not an automatic instruction to change it: context, purpose, and the writer's voice matter. Act on one clear instance of the strongest tells; weak-alone patterns need repetition or supporting tells. Keep deliberate rhetoric, quoted wording, proper names, technical terms, and style that belongs to the writer.

## Ranked patterns

### 1. Staged contrast: “not X but Y”

Watch for “not just/not only/not merely X, but Y,” “it is not X, it is Y,” “X rather than Y” used to inflate a point, and the same contrast split across sentences. Keep a contrast when the first half corrects a real belief or both halves add information. Otherwise state the point directly. This merges humanizer staging with unslop's “not just X but Y” rule.

### 2. Redundant one-line closers and dramatic fragments

Cut a standalone closer that repeats the prior paragraph (“that is the real win,” “that distinction matters”), announces a lesson the example already makes clear, or repeats the same formula after each section. Remove fragments staged for emphasis when they add no claim. Keep a short line when it adds a new fact or consequence.

### 3. Deep-sounding sayings and metaphor-as-insight

Watch for “the real question,” “at its core,” “what really matters,” “the heart of the matter,” “the language/currency/architecture of,” and aphorisms that dress an ordinary point as a hidden truth. Replace with the specific claim. Also inspect mannered flourishes, figurative verbs, personified code, and abstract metaphor nouns in the vocabulary section.

### 4. Staged run-ups

Remove announcements before a routine point: “let's dive in,” “here's what you need to know,” “without further ado,” “quick note,” “honestly?” or “here's the thing” when used as a standalone lead-in. Keep conversational words when they naturally belong inside the sentence or are part of the writer's voice.

### 5. Arguing with an absent opponent

Look for unsupported objections or defenses: “I'm not saying,” “to be clear,” “don't get me wrong,” “some might say,” “you might think,” or a rejected approach no reader raised. Cut the defense and state the claim. Keep a real objection that the text identifies and answers.

### 6. Forced triads and rule-of-three rhythm

Check three-item lists, parallel examples, and paragraph-level sequences. Keep three items when each is distinct and needed. Otherwise use the natural number, develop the strongest example, or vary the structure. Do not remove a real three-part set just to avoid a pattern.

### 7. Repeated sentence openings

Several adjacent sentences beginning with the same subject or phrase may signal template rhythm. Merge, vary the subject, or lead with the action only if it improves the passage. Keep repetition used deliberately for voice or emphasis.

### 8. Dashes as the default connector

Check repeated em dashes, en dashes, spaced dashes, or double hyphens used to link clauses. Prefer the punctuation that states the relationship clearly. Avoid em/en dashes by default unless the writer's sample or intended style uses them; match that sample's rate. Do not alter dashes or hyphens inside code, commands, paths, or URLs. This merges humanizer's dash pattern with unslop's em-dash rule.

### 9. Stacked qualifiers and excessive hedging

Trim layers such as “could potentially possibly,” “might arguably,” and “to be fair, it may perhaps.” Keep uncertainty, scope limits, and caveats that the evidence requires. Ordinary hedging is not a tell by itself. Do not turn uncertain claims into certainty.

### 10. Hyphenated pairs in every position

Check compounds that retain a hyphen after the noun (“the process is well-documented”). Use standard spelling and the target style; usually drop a modifier hyphen after the noun (“well documented”). Weak alone. Do not change words whose accepted spelling requires the hyphen.

### 11. Passive voice and missing actors

Prefer active voice when it makes the actor and action clearer. Name the actor when known and relevant. Passive voice is fine when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or the focus is on the result. Avoid dropped subjects and compressed fragments that make the reader infer who did what. This merges both sources' passive-voice advice.

### 12. Stock AI vocabulary

Inspect common model-favored words, especially when clustered or used abstractly: actually, additionally, align with, bolster, crucial, deep dive, delve, enduring, enhance, garner, highlight, interplay, intricate/intricacies, key, landscape, meticulous, pivotal, robust, showcase, tapestry, testament, underscore, valuable, vibrant. Replace with a plain, exact word when meaning allows. Do not flag an accurate technical use or a word solely because it appears on this list. See “Vocabulary and jargon” for the expanded deduplicated watchlist.

### 13. Inflated significance and generic conclusions

Cut claims that an ordinary fact is “pivotal,” “a testament,” “a key role,” an “enduring legacy,” a “broader movement,” or an “indelible mark” unless the source supports that significance. Avoid stock “challenges and legacy,” “future outlook,” “continues to thrive,” “exciting times ahead,” and “a step in the right direction.” End on the last useful fact or a real, sourced next step. This includes generic conclusions.

### 14. Vague associations and attribution

Replace vague “associated with,” “linked to,” “connected to,” or “in connection with” when the source states the actual relationship. Watch for “experts believe,” “observers say,” “industry reports suggest,” “some critics argue,” unnamed publications, or prestige lists used as evidence. Name the source and what it said only when supported. Otherwise retain necessary uncertainty or remove the unsupported attribution. Never invent a relationship or authority.

### 15. Shallow “-ing” riders

Inspect add-on phrases such as “highlighting,” “underscoring,” “ensuring,” “reflecting,” “symbolizing,” “contributing to,” “cultivating,” “fostering,” “encompassing,” or “showcasing” that claim significance beyond the stated fact. Keep an -ing phrase only when it adds a supported, useful fact. This is the merged superficial -ing pattern from both sources.

### 16. Sales language

Remove promotional adjectives or place-description boilerplate such as “rich,” “profound,” “groundbreaking,” “renowned,” “nestled,” “breathtaking,” “must-visit,” “stunning,” “diverse array,” or “natural beauty” when it is advertising rather than supported description. Say what the subject is and does.

### 17. Borrowed authority

Do not use unnamed experts, vague publication claims, follower counts, or outlet lists as substitutes for evidence. Keep a specific source and its supported statement when useful. A missing citation alone is not a tell, and a citation must not be fabricated.

### 18. Fancy substitutes for “is” and “has”

Replace inflated verbs such as “serves as,” “stands as,” “functions as,” “operates as,” “boasts,” “features,” or “offers” with “is,” “are,” or “has” when that is the plain meaning. Keep verbs that convey a real action or distinction.

### 19. Decorative boldface and inline-header lists

Remove bold applied to every name, acronym, or label without a clear purpose. A vertical list with bold label plus colon that merely restates the sentence often reads templated; convert it to prose or use concise labels that add navigation value. A bold lead-in is fine when formatting genuinely helps and the following sentence adds information.

### 20. Decorative headings and formatting

Use sentence case rather than title case headings by default. Remove emojis or arrows used only as decoration in headings and bullets, repeated horizontal rules, and a top-level title duplicated immediately in the body. Preserve meaningful structure and the writer's chosen format.

### 21. Curly quotation marks

Use the target publication's quote style. Curly quotes are weak evidence alone because editors add them automatically. Do not change literal code, exact quotations, or a format that requires them.

### 22. Chatbot residue and sycophancy

Remove conversational wrapper text from prose that should stand alone: “I hope this helps,” “of course,” “certainly,” “great question,” “you're absolutely right,” “let me know if,” “would you like me to,” and similar praise or offers. Keep a greeting or sign-off when the format is a genuine message. This merges chatbot phrases and sycophantic tone.

### 23. Knowledge-limit disclaimers, guesses, and unsupported hedges

Remove model-centered boilerplate such as “up to my last training update” or “based on available information” when it says nothing useful. If evidence is missing, state exactly what the source does not show or omit the claim. Never fill a gap with a plausible guess. This does not prohibit a relevant, accurate limitation or uncertainty note.

### 24. Heading repeated in the first sentence

When a heading is followed by a sentence that only paraphrases the heading before the real content starts, remove the redundant sentence. Keep it if it contributes a distinct fact.

### 25. Writing about the document instead of its subject

Cut process narration, drafting history, or explanations of visible layout (“the table below compares,” “this section is organized by”). Keep document-level descriptions when the reader needs them, such as a change log, source credit, or a caveat that affects what they should do.

### 26. Wrong reader: re-explaining shared context

In a reply, do not restate the recipient's problem, reproduce commands or evidence they already saw, or bury the decision at the end. Lead with the answer and keep only reasoning that could change the recipient's view. Use conversation context only when visible; if you cannot tell what the reader knows and it matters, ask or leave the text alone.

## Vocabulary and jargon

Use this section as a targeted watchlist, not a banned-word list. Prefer a concrete, plain alternative only when it preserves the meaning. Do not replace technical terms, accurate uses, proper names, quotes, or a writer's deliberate wording mechanically. Deduplicated overlap with the ranked patterns is linked to the relevant pattern above.

- AI-favored vocabulary: additionally, actually, crucial, delve, enduring, enhance, garner, highlight, interplay, intricate/intricacies, meticulous/meticulously, pivotal, robust, showcase, tapestry, testament, underscore, vibrant, valuable, align with, bolster, key, abstract “landscape,” and “deep dive.” Pattern 12 covers the shared stock-word set.
- Abstract metaphor jargon: substrate, wedge/wedge in, vector, locus, vantage, nexus, primitive as a noun, harness as a metaphor, “API surface,” bedrock, scaffolding as a metaphor, modality, paradigm, gold-plating, ratchet as metaphor, evacuate for moving code, endgame, north star, and flywheel. Name the concrete mechanism, direction, constraint, or action instead.
- Fancy “is” verbs: serves as, stands as, functions as, operates as, boasts, features, offers. See pattern 18.
- Plain-word replacements when accurate: use instead of utilize or leverage; help instead of facilitate; many instead of numerous; if instead of in the event that; to instead of in order to; because instead of due to the fact that.
- Filler phrases: remove “it is important to note that” and similar throat-clearing when they add no caveat or claim.
- Synonym cycling: do not rotate among near-synonyms for the same person or thing merely to avoid repetition. Pick the clearest term and repeat it; vary only when the referent or meaning changes.
- False ranges: “from X to Y” is misleading when endpoints are not positions on a meaningful scale. Name the items or describe the actual range.
- Dense sentences: split a sentence when the reader must backtrack to parse it. Keep related details together when the structure remains clear.
- Adverbs and weak verbs: remove adverbs that merely prop up a weak verb. Use a stronger verb or a measured detail when supplied; do not invent a measurement.
- Say what it does: describe a concrete action, fact, instruction, or number, not a feeling or vague promise. If a sentence could fit any product or project unchanged, make it specific using supported details or cut it.
- Over-compression: restore needed articles, subjects, and verbs; expand symbol-speak and unexplained abbreviations when the reader would otherwise have to decode them.
- Mannered prose: replace decorative metaphor, rhetorical fragments, stock framing, and personified code with literal language where that better serves the writer's voice and meaning.
- Colon overuse: use a colon for a real list or explanation, not as a habitual mid-sentence hinge or comparison crutch.

## Editing safeguards

- Every sentence kept must add information, a useful transition, or a voice choice that contributes something new. Cut repetition, not evidence.
- Never invent facts. Preserve every supported claim, name, number, date, quote, citation, ranking, and meaningful uncertainty. Do not strengthen a claim or imply simultaneity, causation, or consensus the source does not establish.
- Do not mistake deliberate style for a tell. Preserve distinctive details, mixed feelings, dated slang, real asides, and quoted text unless the user asks otherwise.
- A phrase in a quotation, title, proper name, code, or text discussing the phrase is not necessarily an active tell.
- Human writing also uses these patterns. A single weak tell is not proof of AI authorship. Score the text's observable patterns, never whether a person or machine wrote it.
